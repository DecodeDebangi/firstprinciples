## The idea

A singly linked list can only be walked forwards, one node at a time, and you don't know how long it is until
you reach the end. Two questions sound like they need more than that:

- *Where is the middle?* You'd normally count the nodes first, then walk halfway.
- *Does the list loop back on itself?* You'd normally remember every node you've seen.

Both can be answered in one walk with no extra memory, using two pointers that start together at the head and
move at different speeds: `slow` moves **one** node per round, `fast` moves **two**.

## Finding the middle

After any number of rounds, `fast` has walked exactly twice as far as `slow`. So at the moment `fast` runs out of
list, `slow` is halfway.

<Viz client:visible approach="middle" />

The loop stops when `fast` can't take two more steps: either `fast` is `none` or `fast.next` is `none`. Check both,
in that order, or reading `fast.next.next` crashes on `none`.

**Odd or even length?** With 5 nodes there's one middle, the 3rd, and that's where `slow` stops. With 6 nodes
there are two middles, the 3rd and the 4th. The version above stops on the **second** one (try `4, 9, 2, 7, 5, 1`
below the player). If you need the first one instead, as when splitting a list into two halves, change the loop
condition to `fast.next ≠ none and fast.next.next ≠ none`.

## Catching a loop

If the list loops, `fast` never reaches an end. Both pointers end up going round the loop, `fast` behind `slow`
(it has lapped round), and each round it gains one node on `slow`.

### Why they always meet

Once both pointers are on the loop, think only about the **gap**: how many nodes `fast` is behind `slow`.

- Each round, `slow` moves 1 node forward and `fast` moves 2. So `fast` gains exactly **1** node per round, and
  the gap shrinks by exactly 1.
- A gap that shrinks by exactly 1 can't skip over 0. When the gap is 1, `fast`'s two steps land on the node `slow`
  has just moved onto.
- The gap starts smaller than the loop's length, so they meet before `slow` finishes one lap of the loop.

That's why the speeds are 1 and 2. With 1 and 3, `fast` would gain 2 per round and could jump straight over
`slow`.

The whole thing takes O(n) time: `slow` reaches the loop within n steps and they meet within one more lap. And
it takes O(1) space: two pointers, no set of visited nodes.

## Where you'll see it

- [Linked List Cycle](/dsa/linked-list-cycle/): exactly the loop check above.
- [Linked List Cycle II](/dsa/linked-list-cycle-ii/): after they meet, one more walk from the head finds where the
  loop *starts*.
- [Palindrome Linked List](/dsa/palindrome-linked-list/): find the middle, then reverse the second half.
- [Remove Nth Node From End of List](/dsa/remove-nth-node-from-end-of-list/): a cousin of the trick. There both
  pointers move at the **same** speed but start a fixed distance apart, so when the front one reaches the end,
  the back one is `n` nodes from it.

## Interview theory

<QA q="Why not move fast three nodes at a time? Wouldn't it be quicker?">
It can jump over `slow`: with a speed difference of 2, a gap of 1 becomes −1 instead of 0, and the pointers can
circle many times without ever landing together. With speeds 1 and 2 the gap shrinks by exactly 1 per round, so it
must reach 0. The total work is O(n) either way.
</QA>

<QA q="What's the time and space cost of finding the middle this way, versus counting first?">
Both are O(n) time and O(1) space, and both make about 1.5n pointer moves: counting walks n nodes and then n / 2
more; fast walks n while slow walks n / 2. The real advantage is that it's one pass that never needs the length,
which matters when the list could loop or is being read as it arrives.
</QA>

<QA q="Where does slow stop when the list has an even number of nodes?">
With the loop condition `fast ≠ none and fast.next ≠ none`, on the second of the two middle nodes. With
`fast.next ≠ none and fast.next.next ≠ none`, on the first. Pick based on what you do next.
</QA>
