## What a palindrome is

A **palindrome** reads the same forwards and backwards: `racecar`, `noon`, `a`. Its first character equals its last,
its second equals its second-last, and so on to the middle. A single character is a palindrome, and so is the empty
string.

Two kinds of question come up. **Is this string a palindrome?** That's a check. **Which parts of this string are
palindromes?** That's a search over its [substrings](/concepts/strings-and-characters/), and it needs a different
move.

## Checking: walk in from the ends

Put one pointer at each end and compare. If the characters match, step both inwards; if they ever differ, it isn't a
palindrome. This is the [two pointers](/concepts/two-pointers/) move.

<Viz client:visible approach="ends" />

About `n / 2` comparisons: **O(n)** time and **O(1)** space, with no reversed copy. [Valid
Palindrome](/dsa/valid-palindrome/) adds skipping over spaces and punctuation, and [Reverse String](/dsa/reverse-string/)
uses the same two pointers to swap instead of compare.

## Searching: every palindrome has a centre

Checking every substring separately is slow: there are about `n² / 2` substrings, and checking each costs up to O(n),
so **O(n³)** in total. The trick is to notice that every palindrome is built around a **centre**:

- A palindrome of **odd** length, like `bacab`, has a middle character (`c`).
- A palindrome of **even** length, like `abba`, has a middle **gap**, between the two `b`s.

So a string of `n` characters has `n` character centres and `n − 1` gap centres: **2n − 1** centres in all.

## Expanding around a centre

From a centre, step outwards one character on each side at a time. As long as the two new ends match, the wider
string is a palindrome too, because its inside already was. The first mismatch, or the end of the string, stops it:
nothing wider around this centre can be a palindrome, since it would contain the mismatched pair.

Type a position like `3` to centre on a character, or `2.5` for the gap between positions 2 and 3.

<Viz client:visible approach="expand" />

Expanding from one centre takes at most `n / 2` steps, so trying all `2n − 1` centres costs **O(n²)** time and only
**O(1)** extra space. Each step also finds a palindrome, so counting palindromes is one expansion step per palindrome
found, plus one stop per centre. That's [Palindromic Substrings](/dsa/palindromic-substrings/), and keeping the widest
one instead is [Longest Palindromic Substring](/dsa/longest-palindromic-substring/).

## Other ways, briefly

- **A table.** `s[i..j]` is a palindrome when `s[i] = s[j]` and `s[i+1..j-1]` is one. Filling a table of answers for
  every `i` and `j`, shortest first, is also O(n²) time, but needs O(n²) memory. That's [dynamic
  programming](/concepts/memoisation-vs-tabulation/).
- **Manacher's algorithm** reuses what earlier expansions found to do the whole search in O(n). It's rarely expected in
  interviews.

## Interview theory

<QA q="How do you check whether a string is a palindrome in O(1) extra space?">
Two pointers, one at each end. Compare, and step both inwards while they match; any mismatch means no. About n / 2
comparisons, and no reversed copy.
</QA>

<QA q="Why are there 2n − 1 centres, and why do gaps count?">
Odd-length palindromes centre on one of the n characters; even-length ones centre on one of the n − 1 gaps between
neighbours. Skipping the gaps would miss every even-length palindrome, like "abba".
</QA>

<QA q="Why can you stop expanding at the first mismatch?">
Any wider string around the same centre contains that mismatched pair at mirrored positions, so it can't be a
palindrome either.
</QA>
