## What a heap is good at

[The first heap read](/concepts/heaps/) shows how a heap keeps its smallest (or largest) item on top, with O(log n)
push and pop. That's the whole tool: a heap answers "what's the best item **right now**?" while items keep arriving
and leaving. Sorting answers the same question once, for a list that never changes. Three patterns turn that one
ability into answers to very different problems.

## Pattern 1: a heap of size k for "top k"

To find the k largest of n items, you don't need them all in order. Keep a **min-heap of at most k items**: push
each new item, and if the heap grows past k, pop its smallest. The heap always holds the k largest seen so far, and
its top is the **kth largest**.

It feels backwards that a min-heap finds the *largest* items. The reason: the item you need to throw away is the
weakest of the current top k, and a min-heap keeps exactly that one on top, ready to pop.

Each item costs O(log k), so the whole pass is **O(n log k)** instead of O(n log n) for sorting, and it only ever
stores k items, which matters when the data arrives as a stream. [Kth Largest Element in an
Array](/dsa/kth-largest-element-in-an-array/) and [K Closest Points to Origin](/dsa/k-closest-points-to-origin/)
are this pattern exactly.

## Pattern 2: merge k sorted lists with a heap of heads

Each sorted list's smallest value is its first one, its **head**. So the smallest value not yet taken is the
smallest of the k heads. Put the heads in a min-heap; pop the smallest, append it to the output, and push the next
value from the same list.

The heap never holds more than k items, one per list, so each of the n values costs O(log k): **O(n log k)** in
all. [Merge k Sorted Lists](/dsa/merge-k-sorted-lists/) does this with linked lists.

## Pattern 3: two heaps for a running median

The **median** is the middle value once the numbers are sorted (or the average of the two middle values when there
are an even number). To keep it up to date while numbers arrive, split them into two halves:

- a **max-heap** for the smaller half, so its top is the biggest of the small numbers,
- a **min-heap** for the larger half, so its top is the smallest of the big numbers.

Keep the halves the same size, or let the smaller half hold one extra. Then the median is always at the tops: the
smaller half's top for an odd count, the average of both tops for an even count.

<Viz client:visible approach="median" />

Each new number is one push, plus at most one move from one heap to the other to restore the sizes:
**O(log n)** per number, and reading the median is O(1). [Find Median from Data
Stream](/dsa/find-median-from-data-stream/) is this pattern.

## Which pattern?

| The question sounds like | Pattern | Cost |
|---|---|---|
| "kth largest", "k most frequent", "k closest" | Heap of size k | O(n log k) |
| "merge k sorted …", "smallest across k streams" | Heap of heads | O(n log k) |
| "median of a stream", "balance two halves" | Two heaps | O(log n) per item |
| "repeatedly take the best remaining" | One heap of everything | O(log n) per step |

## Interview theory

<QA q="Why a min-heap to find the k largest items?">
The heap holds the best k so far. When a new item arrives and the heap grows to k + 1, the one to drop is the
smallest of them, and a min-heap keeps exactly that item on top. Its top is also the answer: the kth largest.
</QA>

<QA q="Heap of size k or sort: when does the heap win?">
Sorting costs O(n log n) and needs everything in memory. The heap costs O(n log k) and stores k items, so it wins
when k is much smaller than n, or when the items arrive one at a time and you can't keep them all.
</QA>

<QA q="In the two-heaps median, why must every new number be compared with a top?">
Each heap must hold only its own half: everything in the smaller half must be at most everything in the larger
half. Comparing the new number with the smaller half's top decides which half it belongs to; the rebalancing move
then only fixes the sizes.
</QA>
