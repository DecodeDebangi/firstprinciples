Some problems ask two things of a list, over and over, in any order: **change one value**, and **add up a range**
of positions. Each is easy alone. Doing both fast is the problem, and two structures built from trees solve it.

The animations here take a list and a few operations: `sum l r` adds up positions `l` to `r`, and `set i v` changes
position `i` to `v`. Try your own in the boxes under each one.

## The trade-off with prefix sums

[Prefix sums](/concepts/prefix-sums/) store `pre[k]`, the total of the first `k` values. A range total is then one
subtraction, `pre[r + 1] − pre[l]`. But every `pre[k]` after a changed position includes the old value, so a change
means fixing up to `n` totals.

<Viz client:visible approach="prefix" />

Keeping the plain list has the opposite problem: a change is one step, but a sum walks the whole range. Either way,
one of the two operations is O(n). The fix is to store totals that each cover **some** of the list, so a change only
touches a few of them and a range is made of a few of them.

## Segment trees: totals for halves, quarters, eighths…

A **segment tree** is a binary tree where each node holds the total of a stretch of positions. The root covers the
whole list, its children each cover half, their children a quarter, down to leaves that cover one position each. The
small label under each node in the animation is its stretch.

- **A range total** starts at the root. A node completely **inside** the range contributes its total and stops; one
  completely **outside** contributes 0 and stops; one that's partly inside asks both children. At most about two
  nodes per level end up used.
- **A change** fixes the leaf, then recomputes each total on the way back up: one node per level.

<Viz client:visible approach="segment" />

The tree is about log₂ n levels tall, so both operations are **O(log n)**. It has about `2n` nodes. The same shape
works for any way of combining two halves where the combined answer comes from the halves' answers: the minimum,
the maximum, a greatest common divisor, a count.

## Fenwick trees: the same speed in one list

A **Fenwick tree** (also called a binary indexed tree) stores the totals in a plain list, `tree[1..n]`, with positions
counted from 1. `tree[k]` holds the total of a stretch that **ends** at position `k` and is `lowbit(k)` long, where
`lowbit(k)` is the value of k's last 1 bit:

| k | binary | lowbit(k) | tree[k] covers |
|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 101 | 1 | 5 |
| 6 | 110 | 2 | 5–6 |
| 8 | 1000 | 8 | 1–8 |
| 12 | 1100 | 4 | 9–12 |

- **The total of the first `k` values** adds `tree[k]`, then jumps to `k − lowbit(k)`, the position just before that
  stretch, and repeats until 0. Each jump clears one 1 bit, so there are at most log₂ n jumps.
- **A change at position `k`** adds the difference to `tree[k]`, then jumps to `k + lowbit(k)`, the next stretch that
  reaches back over `k`, until past the end.
- **A range total** is the total of the first `r + 1` values minus the total of the first `l`.

<Viz client:visible approach="fenwick" />

In code, `lowbit(k)` is `k & −k`: in a number and its negative, the last 1 bit is the only bit that is 1 in both.

## Which one to use

| | Prefix sums | Segment tree | Fenwick tree |
|---|---|---|---|
| Change one value | O(n) | O(log n) | O(log n) |
| Range total | O(1) | O(log n) | O(log n) |
| Memory | n | about 2n nodes | n |
| Works for min or max | No | Yes | Not for any range |
| Code | 3 lines | 25 lines | 10 lines |

- The list **never changes**: prefix sums.
- **Sums or counts** with changes: a Fenwick tree. It's short and quick to write in an interview. It also counts
  things: [Count of Smaller Numbers After Self](/dsa/count-of-smaller-numbers-after-self/) keeps how many of each
  value have been seen.
- **Minimums, maximums**, or anything a subtraction can't undo: a segment tree. A Fenwick tree gets a range by
  subtracting two prefixes, which can't work for a minimum.

Problems: [Range Sum Query - Mutable](/dsa/range-sum-query-mutable/) and [Count of Smaller Numbers After
Self](/dsa/count-of-smaller-numbers-after-self/).

## Interview theory

<QA q="How do you answer range sums on an array that also gets point updates?">
A segment tree or a Fenwick tree: O(log n) per update and per query, O(n) to build. Prefix sums make updates O(n).
</QA>

<QA q="How does a segment tree answer a range query in O(log n)?">
Starting at the root, nodes fully inside the range return their stored total and nodes fully outside return nothing;
only nodes that overlap the range's edges recurse. At most about two nodes per level are used.
</QA>

<QA q="What does tree[k] hold in a Fenwick tree?">
The total of the lowbit(k) positions ending at k, where lowbit(k) = k & −k is k's last 1 bit. A prefix query jumps
k ← k − lowbit(k); an update jumps k ← k + lowbit(k).
</QA>

<QA q="Segment tree or Fenwick tree?">
Fenwick for sums and counts: less code and memory. Segment tree when the combine step can't be undone by subtraction
(min, max, gcd) or when whole ranges are updated at once (with lazy propagation: a node records a pending change and passes it to its
children only when a later operation goes below it).
</QA>
