## The question it answers

Imagine items (computers, people, cells of a grid) and links arriving one at a time: "0 is connected to 1", "2 to 3",
"1 to 3"… At any moment you want to know whether two items are connected, directly or through others, and how many
separate groups there are. A [graph search](/concepts/graphs/) after every new link would work, but repeats a lot of
work. **Union-find** keeps the groups up to date as links arrive.

## Groups as trees

Every item has a **parent**. At the start, each item is its own parent: a group of one, and its own **root**. A group is
a tree of parent pointers, and its root names the group.

- **find(x)** follows parents up from `x` until it reaches an item that is its own parent: the root. Two items are in
  the same group exactly when they have the same root.
- **union(a, b)** finds both roots. If they differ, point one root at the other. That single change moves a whole tree
  into the other group.

## The catch: long chains

A find costs one step per level of its tree. Nothing so far stops a tree from becoming a long chain. Linking `0-1`,
`1-2`, `2-3`, `3-4` one after another, each union puts the old chain under a new root:

<Viz client:visible approach="chain" />

Now `find(0)` walks the whole chain: O(n) per operation, and O(n²) over `n` links.

## Two fixes that make it almost O(1)

- **Union by size.** Keep the size of each group. When joining, hang the root of the **smaller** tree under the root of
  the bigger one. An item only gets one level deeper when its group at least doubles, so no tree is deeper than
  `log₂ n`.
- **Path compression.** While find walks up to the root, point every item it passes straight at the root. The next
  find from any of them takes one step.

Together they make each operation so close to O(1) that it's usually treated as constant. (The exact bound grows with
the inverse Ackermann function, which is below 5 for any input that fits in a computer.)

## Where it's used

- **Counting groups** as links arrive: start with `n` groups and subtract one for each union that joins two different
  roots. That's [Number of Provinces](/dsa/number-of-provinces/) and [Number of Operations to Make Network
  Connected](/dsa/number-of-operations-to-make-network-connected/).
- **Finding a cycle**: in an undirected graph, an edge whose two ends already have the same root closes a loop.
  That's [Redundant Connection](/dsa/redundant-connection/).
- **Merging things that turn out equal**, like the letters in [Satisfiability of Equality
  Equations](/dsa/satisfiability-of-equality-equations/).

Union-find can only join groups, never split them. If links can also be removed, it's the wrong tool.

## Interview theory

<QA q="What are the two operations of union-find, and what do they do?">
find(x) returns the root of x's tree, which identifies its group. union(a, b) finds both roots and, if they differ,
points one at the other, merging the groups.
</QA>

<QA q="What do union by size and path compression each fix?">
Union by size keeps trees shallow (depth at most log₂ n) by hanging the smaller tree under the bigger. Path compression
flattens trees as they're used, by pointing every item a find passes straight at the root. Together, operations are
almost O(1).
</QA>

<QA q="When would you choose union-find over BFS or DFS?">
When connections arrive one at a time and you need to answer "connected?" or "how many groups?" in between, or to spot
the first edge that makes a cycle. A single BFS/DFS is fine when the whole graph is known up front and only asked once.
</QA>
