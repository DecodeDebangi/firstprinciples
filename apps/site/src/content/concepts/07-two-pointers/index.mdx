## The idea

Many array problems are secretly about **pairs**: two numbers that add up to a target, two walls that hold the most
water, two ends of a palindrome. An array of n items has about n²/2 pairs, so checking each one is O(n²).

Two pointers is a way to look at only O(n) of them **and still be sure** you didn't skip the answer. The trick is
that, on the right kind of input, one comparison can rule out a whole group of pairs at once.

## Shape 1: Pointers from both ends

Now sort the array and put one pointer at each end. Look at `a[lo] + a[hi]`:

- **Too small?** `a[hi]` is the largest number left, so pairing `a[lo]` with anything else gives an even smaller
  sum. Every pair in row `lo` is impossible: move `lo` right.
- **Too big?** `a[lo]` is the smallest number left, so `a[hi]` is too big with every partner. Column `hi` is out:
  move `hi` left.

Watch the table: each sum wipes out a whole row or column, including pairs that were never looked at (`–`).

<Viz client:visible approach="ends" />

That's the whole proof that skipping is safe: a pair is only skipped after a comparison has shown it **can't** be
the answer. Every step removes one candidate number, so after at most n − 1 sums, the pointers meet.

## Shape 2: Same direction (read and write)

The second shape has both pointers moving left to right at different speeds. A fast **read** pointer `r` looks at
every item; a slow **write** pointer `w` marks where the next item worth keeping goes. Everything left of `w` is
finished. It compacts, filters or deduplicates an array in place, with no extra array.

<Viz client:visible approach="readwrite" />

[Move Zeroes](/dsa/move-zeroes/) is the same pattern with a different "keep it?" test, and
[Merge Sorted Array](/dsa/merge-sorted-array/) runs it backwards from the end so nothing is overwritten.

## When will two pointers work?

Ask: **after one comparison, can I say for certain that one end is useless?** That needs some kind of order:

- **Sorted values**: a sum that's too small means the smaller number is the problem
  ([Two Sum II](/dsa/two-sum-ii-input-array-is-sorted/)).
- **A limit set by one side**: in [Container With Most Water](/dsa/container-with-most-water/), the shorter wall
  caps the area, so moving the taller one can't help.
- **Symmetry**: a [palindrome](/dsa/valid-palindrome/) must match from both ends inward.

If the array is unsorted and order matters (the original indices of [Two Sum](/dsa/two-sum/)), sorting would
lose information. Use a hash map instead.

## Two pointers vs sliding window

A **sliding window** is the same-direction shape where you care about **everything between** the pointers, not
just the two ends: the longest substring with no repeated letter, the smallest subarray with a large enough sum.
The right pointer grows the window, the left pointer shrinks it when a rule breaks, and each pointer only ever
moves forward, so it's O(n) too. [Longest Substring Without Repeating Characters](/dsa/longest-substring-without-repeating-characters/)
is the classic example.

## Interview theory

<QA q="Why is the both-ends version O(n) and not O(n²)?">
Every iteration moves `lo` right or `hi` left by one, and they start n − 1 apart. So the loop runs at most n − 1
times. Each iteration does O(1) work.
</QA>

<QA q="Two Sum on an unsorted array: sort and use two pointers, or use a hash map?">
The hash map is O(n) time and O(n) space, and keeps the original indices. Sorting first costs O(n log n) and moves
the items, so you'd have to carry the original indices along. Two pointers wins only when the input is already
sorted or space must be O(1).
</QA>

<QA q="How does 3Sum use two pointers?">
Sort the array, fix the first number `a[i]`, then run the both-ends search on the rest for target `−a[i]`. That's
n searches of O(n) each: O(n²), down from O(n³). Skip repeated values of `a[i]` to avoid duplicate triples.
</QA>

<QA q="What's a fast and slow pointer on a linked list?">
Two pointers moving at different speeds: slow moves one node, fast moves two. When fast reaches the end, slow is at
the middle. If the list has a cycle, fast eventually laps slow and they meet (Floyd's algorithm).
</QA>
