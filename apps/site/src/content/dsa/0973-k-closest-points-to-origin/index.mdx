## The problem

Given a list of points `(x, y)` with whole-number coordinates and a number `k`, return the `k` points closest to
the origin `(0, 0)`, in any order. Distance is the usual straight-line distance, √(x² + y²).

For `(3, 1)`, `(−1, 2)`, `(4, −3)`, `(0, 1)`, `(−2, −2)`, `(2, 0)` and `k = 3`, the closest three are `(0, 1)`,
`(2, 0)` and `(−1, 2)`.

## The key question

> **I need the k smallest distances, not every distance in order. How do I track just those k while walking the
> points once?**

First, a shortcut: a bigger x² + y² always means a bigger √(x² + y²), so compare **x² + y²** and skip the square
root. It's exact with whole numbers, and faster.

## Approach 1: Sort every point by distance

Sort the points by x² + y² and return the first `k`.

<Viz client:visible approach="sort" />

<Complexity time="O(n log n)" space="O(1) to O(n)">
**Time.** Sorting n points: **O(n log n)**.

**Space.** Depends on the sort: **O(1)** in place, up to **O(n)** for many built-in sorts.

**What it wastes:** it puts all n points in order, when only the k closest matter and their order doesn't.
</Complexity>

## Approach 2: A max-heap of the k closest

Keep the k closest points seen so far in a **max-heap** ordered by x² + y². Push each point; if the heap holds
k + 1, pop the top, the farthest of them. It's the [top-k pattern](/concepts/heaps-in-practice/) turned around:
to keep the **smallest** distances, the heap keeps the **largest** of them on top, ready to drop.

<Viz client:visible approach="heap" />

<Complexity time="O(n log k)" space="O(k)">
**Time.** One push and at most one pop per point, on a heap of at most k + 1: **O(n log k)**.

**Space.** The heap holds at most k + 1 points: **O(k)**.

**What it wastes:** when k is close to n, the heap saves little over sorting. Quickselect (partitioning by
distance and keeping only the side with the boundary) is O(n) on average but O(n²) at worst.
</Complexity>

## Common mistakes

- **Using a min-heap of size k.** Its top is the closest point, but the one to drop is the farthest: use a max-heap.
- **Taking square roots.** They're slower and can introduce rounding; x² + y² gives the same order.
- **Sorting the answer.** The problem accepts any order; sorting the k results adds work for nothing.
- **Comparing |x| + |y|.** That's a different distance: `(3, 0)` and `(2, 2)` disagree under the two measures.
