## The problem

You get a list `heights`: the heights of bars standing side by side, each 1 unit wide. Return the **area** of the
largest rectangle that fits entirely under the bars.

For `heights = [2, 4, 3, 5, 1, 3]` the answer is `9`: a rectangle of height 3 across the bars at positions 1, 2 and 3
(heights 4, 3 and 5), so 3 × 3.

## The key question

> **For each bar, how far can a rectangle of exactly its height stretch left and right?**

Until the nearest shorter bar on each side. The largest rectangle has the height of its shortest bar, so it is one
of those `n` rectangles, one per bar. Finding the nearest shorter bars is a monotonic stack's job.

## Approach 1: Try every left and right edge

For every left edge `i`, move the right edge `j` from `i` to the end. The rectangle can be as tall as the shortest bar
between them, so keep that minimum up to date as `j` moves.

<Viz client:visible approach="pairs" />

<Complexity time="O(n²)" space="O(1)">
**Time.** Every pair `i ≤ j` is tried, each in O(1) thanks to the running minimum: `n(n + 1)/2` pairs, **O(n²)**.

**Space.** A few variables: **O(1)**.

**What it wastes:** most pairs can't be the answer. Only the `n` rectangles that stretch as far as each bar's height
allows matter.
</Complexity>

## Approach 2: Find each bar's shorter neighbours, then measure

Find, for every bar, the nearest shorter bar on the left and on the right. One stack pass from the left gives the left
ones: pop bars at least as tall, and whatever is left on top is the answer. A second pass from the right gives the
right ones. Then each bar's rectangle is its height × (right − left − 1).

<Viz client:visible approach="twoPass" />

<Complexity time="O(n)" space="O(n)">
**Time.** Each pass pushes every bar once and pops it at most once, and the final loop is `n` steps: **O(n)**.

**Space.** The `left` and `right` arrays and the stack: **O(n)**.

**What it wastes:** two passes and two extra arrays, when one pass can find both neighbours at once.
</Complexity>

## Approach 3: One pass, where a pop knows both edges (optimal)

Keep bars on a stack with heights rising toward the top. When a new bar is no taller than the top, pop the top: the
new bar is the first shorter bar on its right, and the bar now under it is the first shorter bar on its left. So its
rectangle is measured right there. To empty the stack at the end, treat the position after the last bar as a bar of
height 0. [Monotonic stack patterns](/concepts/monotonic-stack-patterns/) walks through why the stack stays in order.

<Viz client:visible approach="stack" />

<Complexity time="O(n)" space="O(n)">
**Time.** Each bar is pushed once and popped once, and each pop measures one rectangle in O(1): **O(n)**.

**Space.** If heights only rise, every bar waits on the stack: **O(n)**.
</Complexity>

## Comparing the approaches

Race two approaches on the same input. Both share one clock that ticks once per operation, so the one doing extra work visibly falls behind.

<Compare client:visible />

| Approach | Time | Space | Idea |
|---|---|---|---|
| Every pair of edges | O(n²) | O(1) | Running minimum between the edges |
| Two stack passes | **O(n)** | O(n) | Nearest shorter bar on each side, then measure |
| One stack pass | **O(n)** | O(n) | Each pop knows both of its blockers |

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting the bars left on the stack.** Bars that never meet a shorter bar still need measuring; the pretend bar
  of height 0 at the end pops them all.
- **Measuring the width from the popped bar.** The rectangle starts just after the bar *under* it on the stack, not at
  the popped bar itself, because the bars popped earlier between them were all taller.
- **Worrying about equal heights.** With `≥`, the first of two equal bars is measured too narrow, but the second
  measures the full width, so the answer is still right.
