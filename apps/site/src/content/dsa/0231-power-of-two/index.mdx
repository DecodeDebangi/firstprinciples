## The problem

You get a whole number `n`, which may be negative. Return whether it's a power of two: `2^k` for some whole `k ≥ 0`.
The powers of two are 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, …

For example, 16 is (`2^4`), but 48 isn't: `48 = 16 · 3`. Zero and negative numbers never are.

## The key question

> **What do all powers of two have in common, written in binary?**

See [Binary](/concepts/binary-and-bits/): a power of two is `1` followed only by `0`s, like `10000`.

## Approach 1: Halve while even

A power of two is 1 doubled `k` times. So undo it: while `n` is even, halve it. If what's left is 1, it was a power of
two; any other odd number means some other factor was in there.

<Viz client:visible approach="halve" />

<Complexity time="O(log n)" space="O(1)">
**Time.** Each halving removes one bit, so at most `log₂ n` halvings: **O(log n)**, at most 31 for a 32-bit number.

**Space.** One number: **O(1)**.

**What it wastes:** it checks one bit per loop. The question "is there exactly one 1?" can be answered for all the bits
at once.
</Complexity>

## Approach 2: Exactly one 1 bit (optimal)

`n & (n − 1)` removes the lowest 1 bit (see [Bit tricks](/concepts/bit-tricks/)). If `n` has a single 1, nothing is
left. So `n` is a power of two exactly when `n > 0` and `n & (n − 1) = 0`. The `n > 0` part matters: `0 & −1` is also
0.

<Viz client:visible approach="oneBit" />

<Complexity time="O(1)" space="O(1)">
**Time.** One subtraction, one AND, one comparison: **O(1)**.

**Space.** **O(1)**.
</Complexity>

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting zero and negatives.** `0 & (0 − 1)` is 0, so the test needs `n > 0`.
- **Using floating-point logarithms.** `log₂(n)` being a whole number seems neat, but rounding errors can make it wrong
  for large `n`.
- **Operator precedence.** In C, C++ and Java, `n & (n - 1) == 0` compares first and ANDs second. Bracket it:
  `(n & (n - 1)) == 0`.
