## The problem

A **circular** linked list is one whose last node points back to the first, so following `next` goes round and
round forever. This one is sorted going round: the values rise, drop once from the largest back to the smallest,
then rise again. You're handed a pointer to *some* node in the ring, not necessarily the smallest, and a value
`x`. Insert a new node holding `x` so that the ring stays sorted, and return the node you were handed. If the ring
is empty, make a ring of just the new node (pointing to itself) and return that.

For example, the ring `5 → 7 → 1 → 3 → (back to 5)`, handed to you at the `5`, with `x = 4`: the new node goes
between the `3` and the `5`, giving `5 → 7 → 1 → 3 → 4 → (back to 5)`.

## The key question

> **There's no first node and no last node. How do I recognise the right gap, including when x is bigger or
> smaller than everything?**

In an ordinary sorted list you'd walk from the head until the next value is at least `x`. In a ring there's no
head to start from, and a new largest or smallest value belongs at the one place where the order wraps around.

## Approach 1: Find the largest first, then walk

First walk forward while the values keep rising, to find the **largest** node: its `next` is the smallest, so
this is where the ring "starts". Then:

- If `x` is at least the largest, or at most the smallest, it goes right there, between the largest and the
  smallest.
- Otherwise it belongs in the middle: start at the smallest and walk, as in an ordinary sorted list, until the next
  value is at least `x`.

<Viz client:visible approach="largestFirst" />

<Complexity time="O(n)" space="O(1)">
**Time.** Finding the largest walks at most once round the ring, and the second walk at most once more: up to
**2n** steps, **O(n)**.

**Space.** A couple of pointers and the one new node: **O(1)**.

**What it wastes:** it can walk most of the ring twice: once just to find the largest, then again to the gap.
</Complexity>

## Approach 2: One pass, three cases (optimal)

Look at each neighbouring pair `prev → curr` once, starting from the node you were handed. Stop at the first pair
where `x` belongs. There are exactly three ways that can happen:

1. **It fits between them:** `prev.val ≤ x ≤ curr.val`.
2. **It's at the drop:** `prev.val > curr.val` means you're standing at the wrap from largest to smallest. If `x`
   is at least the largest or at most the smallest, this is its place.
3. **You've gone all the way round** without stopping. That only happens when every value is the same, so any
   gap is fine.

Then link the node in: `prev.next ← node` and `node.next ← curr`.

<Viz client:visible approach="onePass" />

<Complexity time="O(n)" space="O(1)">
**Time.** Each of the n pairs is checked at most once: **O(n)**.

**Space.** Two pointers and the new node: **O(1)**.
</Complexity>

## Comparing the approaches

Both on the same ring:

<Compare client:visible />

| Approach | Time | Space | Walks round |
|---|---|---|---|
| Find the largest, then walk | O(n) | O(1) | up to twice |
| One pass, three cases | O(n) | O(1) | at most once |

Both are O(n), so there's no growth chart. The one-pass version does at most half the walking, and needs no
separate "find the start" step.

## Common mistakes

- **Assuming you were handed the smallest node.** You might be anywhere in the ring, even right before the drop.
- **Forgetting the drop case.** Without case 2, a new largest or smallest value never finds a gap where
  `prev.val ≤ x ≤ curr.val`, and the walk goes round forever.
- **Looping forever when all values are equal.** With a ring like `2 → 2 → 2` and `x = 5`, neither case 1 nor case
  2 ever matches. Stop when `prev` is back where it started.
- **Forgetting the empty ring.** With no nodes at all, the new node must point to itself.
