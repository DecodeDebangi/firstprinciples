## The problem

You have a pile of stones, each with a whole-number weight. Each round, take the **two heaviest** stones, `y` and
`x` with `y ≥ x`, and smash them together:

- If they weigh the same, both are destroyed.
- Otherwise `x` is destroyed and `y` becomes a stone of weight `y − x`, which goes back on the pile.

Stop when at most one stone is left. Return its weight, or `0` if none is left.

For stones `[3, 9, 4, 6, 2, 5]`: smash 9 and 6 (3 goes back), then 5 and 4 (1 goes back), then 3 and 3 (both gone),
then 2 and 1. The answer is `1`.

## The key question

> **How do I keep getting the two largest items from a collection that keeps changing?**

Sorting answers "what are the largest?" once. But every round adds a new stone, so the order breaks and you'd have to
sort again. A **heap** is built for exactly this: take the largest in O(log n), add a new item in O(log n).

## Approach 1: Re-sort every round

Sort the pile, take the last two, push the remainder back, repeat.

<Viz client:visible approach="sort" />

<Complexity time="O(n² log n)" space="O(1) extra">
**Time.** Each round removes at least one stone, so there are at most `n − 1` rounds, and each one sorts up to `n`
stones: O(n log n). Together: **O(n² log n)**. (Some library sorts, like Timsort in Python and Java, are fast on an almost-sorted
list, but you can't rely on that in an interview.)

**Space.** Sorting in place: **O(1) extra**, beyond whatever buffer the sort itself uses.

**What it wastes:** after one round, the pile is still almost sorted, yet the whole thing is sorted again from scratch.
</Complexity>

## Approach 2: Max-heap (optimal)

A **max-heap** keeps the heaviest stone at the top and only partly orders the rest: every parent is at least as heavy
as its children. That's just enough order to pop the heaviest and repair the heap in O(log n). The tree and the array
below are the same heap: item `i`'s children sit at `2i + 1` and `2i + 2`.

In your language: C++'s `priority_queue` is a max-heap already, Java's `PriorityQueue` becomes one with
`Collections.reverseOrder()`, and Python's `heapq` is min-only, so there you store negated weights (the most negative
number is the heaviest stone).

<Viz client:visible approach="heap" />

<Complexity time="O(n log n)" space="O(n)">
**Time.** `heapify` is O(n). Each of the at most `n − 1` rounds does two pops and at most one push, each
O(log n): **O(n log n)** overall.

**Space.** The heap holds up to `n` stones: **O(n)** (or O(1) extra if you negate the input list in place).
</Complexity>

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting to negate on the way out.** `heappop` returns the negated weight; negate it again before comparing
  or returning.
- **Pushing a zero.** When the two stones are equal, nothing goes back. Pushing `0` gives the right answer here but
  adds a pointless round.
- **Returning `heap[0]` on an empty heap.** If every stone was destroyed, return `0`.
- **Using `heap[0]` and `heap[1]` as the two largest.** Only the top is guaranteed; the second largest is one of the
  top's children, so pop twice.
