## The problem

You get a list of non-negative whole numbers. Return the largest value of `nums[i] xor nums[j]` over any two of them.

For `3, 10, 5, 12, 9` the answer is 15: 3 xor 12 = 0011 xor 1100 = 1111. (New to XOR? [Binary and bits](/concepts/binary-and-bits/) explains it.)

## The key question

> **What decides which of two candidate partners gives the bigger XOR?**

The highest bit where their XORs differ. A 1 in bit b is worth more than every lower bit together, so the best partner is the one that differs from x in the highest bits possible. A trie of bits finds it from the top down.

## Approach 1: Try every pair

XOR every pair of numbers and keep the largest.

<Viz client:visible approach="pairs" />

<Complexity time="O(n²)" space="O(1)">
**Time.** n(n − 1)/2 pairs: **O(n²)**.

**Space.** Nothing extra: **O(1)**.

**What it wastes:** pairs that can't win: anything whose XOR has a 0 in a bit where some other pair has a 1.
</Complexity>

## Approach 2: A binary trie: the best partner for each number (optimal)

Insert every number as B bits, highest first. Then for each number, walk down: at each bit, take the child with the opposite bit if it exists (adding 2^b to the XOR), otherwise the same bit. Keep the best result.

<Viz client:visible approach="trie" />

<Complexity time="O(n · B)" space="O(n · B)">
**Time.** Each number is inserted and walked in B steps: **O(n · B)**, with B ≤ 31.

**Space.** The trie: up to **n · B** nodes.
</Complexity>

## Comparing the approaches

Race two approaches on the same input. Both share one clock that ticks once per operation, so the one doing extra work visibly falls behind.

## Common mistakes

- **Storing bits lowest first.** The greedy choice only works from the highest bit down.
- **Giving numbers different lengths.** Pad every number to the same B bits, or the paths won't line up.
- **Following the same bit when the opposite exists.** The opposite bit is always at least as good.
